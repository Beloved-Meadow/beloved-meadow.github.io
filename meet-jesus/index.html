Meet Jesus

Who is Jesus?

You can read this aloud in about five minutes. It’s for anyone meeting Jesus for the first time, or for the first time in a while.

A real personA baby, a carpenter, a friend

About two thousand years ago, a baby was born in a small town called Bethlehem. His mother was called Mary. He grew up in a village called Nazareth and worked as a carpenter (Mark 6:3). He had friends. He got tired after long walks (John 4:6). He went to a wedding and helped when the wine ran out (John 2:1–11). His name was Jesus.

Jesus was a real person, like you. But the Bible says he is more than that. He is God’s Son. He is God, who came close and lived a real human life so that people could see what God is like (John 1:14, 18).

Pause here. Is there anything you’d like to ask before we go on?

What he didJesus went towards people

When Jesus grew up, he walked from town to town telling people that God’s kingdom had come near (Mark 1:14–15). And he showed them what that meant.

  • He reached out and touched a man with a skin disease, someone other people kept away from, and healed him (Mark 1:40–42).
  • He ate dinner with people that others called “sinners” (Mark 2:15–17).
  • He woke up in a storm and calmed the wind and the waves (Mark 4:35–41).
  • He took children in his arms when his friends tried to send them away (Mark 10:13–16).
  • He told a story about a shepherd who leaves his ninety-nine sheep to look for the one that is lost (Luke 15:3–7).

Everywhere Jesus went, he went towards people: lonely people, ill people, frightened people, people who had done wrong things.

Why he cameSomething was wrong that we couldn’t fix

Everyone does wrong things. We say unkind words, we take what isn’t ours, we leave people out, we pretend. The Bible calls this sin. Sin isn’t just breaking a rule. It spoils things between us and other people, and between us and God. We can’t make it go away by trying harder.

Jesus came to put that right. In Mark’s Gospel, Jesus says why he came:

“For the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.”

Mark 10:45, World English Bible. “Son of Man” is a name Jesus used for himself.

A ransom is a price paid to set someone free. Jesus was saying that he would give his own life to set us free.

The cross and the empty tombJesus died, and Jesus rose

Some powerful people hated what Jesus said and did. One night he was arrested. The next day he was nailed to a wooden cross, and he died. His friends were heartbroken. They put his body in a tomb cut into the rock and rolled a stone across the door.

Three days later, early in the morning, some women who loved Jesus went to the tomb. The stone had been rolled away and his body wasn’t there. Two messengers from God said to them:

“Why do you seek the living among the dead? He isn’t here, but is risen.”

Luke 24:5–6, World English Bible

Over the next weeks, Jesus met his friends again. He talked with them, he ate with them and they touched his hands (Luke 24:36–43). He was really alive.

What it meansForgiveness, hope and life with God

One of Jesus’ first followers, Paul, wrote down the most important news of all:

“…that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures.”

1 Corinthians 15:3–4, World English Bible
  • Forgiveness. When Jesus died, he carried the weight of our wrong things. Anyone who trusts him can be forgiven and start again, every time.
  • Hope. Jesus came back to life, so death isn’t the end. One day God will make everything new.
  • Life with God. Through Jesus you can know God as your Father, who loves you. You can talk to him anytime, about anything.

TodayJesus is alive now

You can’t see Jesus the way his friends did. But you can talk to him, and you can get to know him by hearing what he said and did. That’s what the eight readings below are for. Each one is about a question that children really ask.

One thing to remember

Jesus is God come close. He died for us, he rose again, and he wants to be known.

Eight readings to share

Each one takes about three minutes to read aloud. Read them in any order, one a day or one a week. Readings 1–4 are a good start. Readings 5–8 go on to the cross and the empty tomb.

  • Reading 1 · John 14:8–9

    What is God like?

    Look at Jesus to see the Father.

  • Reading 2 · Mark 10:13–16

    Does Jesus have room for me?

    Jesus calls the children closer.

  • Reading 3 · Luke 19:1–10

    Can Jesus still love me when I’ve done something wrong?

    Jesus goes to Zacchaeus’s house.

  • Reading 4 · Mark 14:32–36

    Can I talk to God when I’m scared?

    Jesus prays honestly in the garden.

  • Reading 5 · Mark 10:46–52

    Can I ask Jesus for what I need?

    Jesus stands still for Bartimaeus.

  • Reading 6 · John 21:15–17

    What if I let Jesus down?

    Breakfast on the beach with Peter.

  • Reading 7 · John 10:11–15

    Why did Jesus die?

    The good shepherd who stayed.

  • Reading 8 · John 20:24–29

    Is Jesus really alive?

    Jesus comes back for Thomas.

For the grown-up: how to use these readings
  • Read slowly and leave the pauses in. When a reading says “pause”, count to ten in your head. Children often notice the most interesting thing at about seven.
  • Ask, then listen. Many questions have more than one good answer. If your child’s answer surprises you, try “Tell me more about that” before you explain anything.
  • “I don’t know” is a fine answer, from them or from you. You can say, “Let’s keep wondering about that.”
  • Prayer is always a choice. Your child can pray aloud, pray in their own words, pray silently or not pray today. What they say to God stays between them and God unless they choose to share it.
  • For every age. With younger children, read just the moment, the Bible text and one question, and use the borrowed prayer. With older children and teenagers, ask all the questions and invite them to read the Bible text aloud themselves or look it up in their own Bible.
  • Translation. Quotations are from the World English Bible, which is in the public domain. Feel free to read from your own Bible instead. Retellings are clearly labelled.
